# Naming — round four

Supersedes the coined round (Marela, Nolera, Nemba, Tanela, Tovela, Kembo), which
was rejected outright. Supersedes Relais, Meanwhile and Carnet before it.

Four rounds is enough to stop blaming the candidates. The list was bad because the
method was bad, and the method was bad in a way that can be demonstrated rather than
argued about. That demonstration is most of what this document is for.

## Why round three produced mush

Round three ran two filters. Both are defensible on their own. Together they can only
produce one kind of name, and it is the forgettable kind.

**Filter one: the bilingual phonetic rule.** A candidate only qualified if an English
speaker and a French speaker say it the same way on sight. That excludes `u`, `ou`,
`au`, `ai`, `ei`, `c`, `g`, `h`, `j`, `w`, `x`, `y`, `th`, `ch` and `ph`, leaving the
consonant set `m n l r v s t p k b d f`.

Look at what survives. Four of the twelve are liquids and nasals — `m n l r` — and
they are the letters that combine most freely with the surviving vowels, so they
dominate every string the generator produces. Liquids and nasals are the *softest*
sounds in the language. They have no burst, no edge and nothing for the ear to catch
on. That is why Marela, Nolera, Tanela and Tovela are almost interchangeable: they
are made of the same four sounds in a different order.

The evidence for this is sitting in the round-three shortlist itself. Of the six, the
only two anyone could hold in their head were **Nemba** and **Kembo** — and the thing
those two share, and the other four lack, is a nasal-plus-stop cluster. `mb` is a
tiny percussive event. It is the entire difference.

**Filter two: the App Store exact-match test.** A candidate was rejected if any app
already led with that word. This sounds like diligence. What it actually does is
described by the number below.

## The number that changes the brief

I screened **80 ordinary, concrete, picturable words** against the App Store in the
US, French and UK storefronts — everything from *Daybook* and *Larder* to *Cairn*,
*Tandem*, *Threshold*, *Bindle* and *Understudy*. Not invented strings. Real words
that carry a picture.

**Seventy-nine of the eighty were already taken.**

The one that came back clear in all three stores was *Doubleback*, which returned
literally zero results for any related term — because nobody searches for it, which
is the opposite of a recommendation.

So the exact-match test is not a quality filter. **It is a filter that only
meaningless strings can pass**, because meaningless strings are the only thing left
that nobody has used. Run any naming process through it and it will hand you
Marela every time, no matter how good the ideas going in were. That is the
mechanical reason four rounds have failed, and it is not a matter of taste.

## Where good names actually come from

The premise of round three — "it doesn't have to mean anything" — is the part worth
challenging, because the evidence runs the other way.

Meaningless names do work. Kodak, Xerox and Häagen-Dazs are all pure invention
carrying no meaning whatsoever. But every one of them was made memorable by *decades
of enormous advertising*. A name with no picture in it is a name you have to buy
meaning for, and the price is set by how much attention you can purchase. Monzo,
Klarna and Figma did the same thing more cheaply, but still with venture money and
years of exposure.

The names that stick without that budget do one of two things.

**They borrow a picture the listener already owns.** Apple, Amazon, Stripe, Slack,
Oracle, Nike, Oatly, Bumble, Hinge, Peanut, Calm, Hatch. The listener does not have
to memorise anything, because they already know the word. All the brand has to do is
attach a second meaning to a hook that is already in the listener's head. This is
overwhelmingly the most common shape among names that got famous cheaply.

**Or they are one small step off a real word.** Google from googol, Flickr from
flicker, Lyft from lift, Nanit from nanny, Owlet from owl, Etsy from a syllable
overheard in a Fellini film. Near-words keep the picture and gain ownability, which
is the trick round three was reaching for and missed by making the step too large.

There is also a phonetic layer under both. Stop consonants — `p t k b d g` — are
easier to recall than continuants, and the reason is physical: a stop is a discrete
event with a beginning and an end, so the ear gets a foothold. This is why the
memorable invented names are *gritty* rather than smooth. Kodak, Xerox, Zoom, TikTok,
Klarna, Venmo, Figma, Bento, Vinted. Not one of them is made of `m n l r`.

## The test that actually matters

The brief, in your words: *"a friend mentioning the app in passing needs to stick."*
That is a good test and it can be run properly. The sentence is:

> "You should get **____**. You write everything down once and just send it to
> whoever's got the kids."

Three questions about it:

1. **Does the listener have anything to hold?** Either they already know the word, or
   the sound is odd enough to replay. Marela gives them nothing and is gone in ten
   seconds.
2. **Can they spell it the next day?** This is where word-of-mouth actually dies. If
   they cannot type it into a search box, the recommendation was worth nothing.
3. **Does the name survive being explained?** The very best case is that it does not
   need explaining, because hearing it tells you what the thing does.

And then, separately, the ownability questions — which are *not* "is it clear on the
App Store", because we now know that filter rejects everything:

4. Is a **serious business in an adjacent category** using it?
5. Is the **trademark** free in Class 9 and Class 42?
6. Can the search result realistically be **won**? A word returning three apps is
   winnable. A word returning fifty is not, even if the exact token is free.

## What the screening found

The full runs are reproducible with `design/name-screen.py`, which checks all three
storefronts and reports how contested the *term* is, not just whether the exact
token is used. Two findings worth keeping.

**The English real-word space is gone.** 79 of 80. This is not a reason to abandon
real words. It is a reason to stop treating a shared token as disqualifying, because
if it were, nothing would qualify.

**The French real-word space is wide open.** Every French word screened came back
clear in all three stores, most with very little noise: *Consigne*, *Escale*,
*Bercail*, *Nichoir*. The App Store is saturated with English and almost nobody names
a consumer app in French. For a product that has to work in France, that is a real
and unexploited asset — the ownability problem and the French-market problem have the
same solution.

## The shortlist

Four candidates, deliberately built on four *different* logics rather than four
variations of one. Each makes a different trade, stated plainly.

### 1. Understudy — the one that explains itself

An understudy is the person who has learned your part so the show can go on without
you. That is not a metaphor for this product; it is a description of it. The
grandparent, the nanny and the dog sitter are understudies, and the app is how they
learn the part.

This is the only candidate that passes question 3 outright. A friend says "it's
called Understudy" and there is nothing left to explain. It also happens to describe
the *emotional* proposition rather than the mechanism: an understudy is someone you
have prepared, so you can leave.

- **App Store:** contested but negligible — one small app, *Understudy: For actors*,
  and only 14 related results in total. Low noise, winnable.
- **Costs:** four syllables and ten letters, which is long on an icon. And it is
  English with no French equivalent — a French speaker manages it but it will never
  feel like theirs. This is the real price.
- **Note:** this appeared in an earlier shortlist and was not chosen. It is back
  because it is the only candidate that wins the test you actually named, and
  because the reason it lost last time — the bilingual rule — is the same rule that
  produced the round nobody liked.

### 2. Consigne — the French word for exactly this

*Les consignes* is the word a French parent already uses for the instructions they
leave behind. *"J'ai laissé les consignes."* It is also *la consigne*, the
left-luggage office — the place you leave something in someone's keeping and collect
it later, intact. Both meanings are the product.

The English reading is the quiet win. An English speaker sees *consigne* and reads
*consign*, which means to place something into another's care. Same Latin root,
correct meaning, arrived at from the other direction.

- **App Store:** clear in all three storefronts.
- **Costs:** *consigne* has an instructional edge in French — a consigne is something
  you are told to follow — which is a colder register than this product wants. And an
  English speaker hearing "con-seen" may not spell it. Needs checking with actual
  French speakers, which I cannot do from here.

### 3. Bento — the box of everything, prepared and handed over

A bento is a meal made by someone who loves you, portioned into compartments, sealed,
and handed over for the day ahead. The picture is exact and it is a picture almost
everyone in both markets already owns.

Phonetically it is close to ideal: two syllables, trochaic, two stop consonants, and
the `nt` cluster that gives it an edge. Identical in English and French, because both
borrowed it from Japanese. Spells itself from hearing, in both.

- **App Store:** contested. Several small apps and *Bento for Creators*; also a
  link-in-bio tool at bento.me and a defunct FileMaker product.
- **Costs:** the food meaning is strong and may fight the product. And a European app
  taking a Japanese word invites a criticism that is easy to make and tedious to
  answer.

### 4. Holdall — the plainest option

A holdall is a bag that holds everything. The name says what the product does using a
word that already exists, which is the Amazon/Stripe move at its most literal.

- **App Store:** the most ownable thing found in the entire exercise. Three results
  across all storefronts. Essentially an empty search space.
- **Costs:** unmistakably British, and meaningless to a French speaker. And it sits
  one vowel away from **Held**, which you have already rejected — the echo may be
  enough to kill it on its own.

## What I would do

**Understudy**, and accept the French cost.

The reasoning is that the two problems are not equal in size. A name that a French
speaker finds slightly foreign is a friction. A name that nobody remembers after
hearing it is a dead product, because word of mouth is the entire distribution plan
for a product like this — there is no advertising budget that makes Marela mean
something. Between a name that half your market finds mildly foreign and a name that
nobody in either market can recall, the first is not a close call.

It is also worth being honest that "works in English and French" has been the
binding constraint in all four rounds, and it is the constraint that produced every
name you have disliked. It is not free. If it holds, **Consigne** is the strongest
French-first answer and the ownability is genuinely excellent — but it needs a
reaction from French parents before anyone bets on it, because I cannot tell from
here whether *consigne* lands as warm or as bureaucratic.

## The decision

Only one thing is needed from you, and it is upstream of the name:

**Is bilingual-native a requirement or a preference?**

- If it is a **requirement**, the answer is Consigne, subject to a reaction check
  with French parents.
- If it is a **preference**, the answer is Understudy.

Everything else follows. The code carries no product name anywhere —
`packages/core` is deliberately name-free and the app renders its name from a single
place — so applying a decision is a one-line change plus the manifest, and nothing
in the build is waiting on it.
