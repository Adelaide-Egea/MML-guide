# Who this is for, and whether it is partnership-ready

Written in answer to two questions asked together: *"how certain are we that a grandparent would
understand how to work this? I feel like this is more tailored to nannies"* and *"I want to be
able to partner with apps like Koru Kids and maybe even Bright Horizons — for that it once again
needs to be really visually appealing, or is this just for direct consumer?"*

They look like one question about polish. They are two different questions, and only one of them
is about design.

## 1. The grandparent worry is correct, and it is not a design problem

The instinct is right, but the reason is not the interface. A grandparent is not confused by this
app. A grandparent has never heard of it.

Every screen in the product is read by someone who did not install it, did not choose it, and did
not agree to it. That is not a usability problem to be solved with bigger buttons — it is a
consent-and-context problem, and it is the single largest risk to the whole product. The nanny
reads it because it arrived attached to a job. The grandparent reads it because their daughter
sent a link at 7am on the way to the airport, and whether they open it at all depends entirely on
what that link looks like when it lands.

Three things follow, and they matter more than any visual pass:

**The guide has to work with no app and no account.** It already does — everything is local-first
and the print path produces the full document including the safety block, whatever was folded on
screen. That is the grandparent's version, and it should stay a first-class output rather than a
downgrade. A PDF that arrives complete is better for that reader than an app that arrives empty.

**The first thing a recipient sees cannot be a product.** It has to be the parent. A link that
opens on "Margaret — from Claire" is a note from your daughter. A link that opens on a brand,
a sign-up, or an onboarding carousel is marketing, and it gets closed.

**Reading must never require an account.** Any authentication wall in front of a caregiver is a
guide that does not get read. The account belongs to the parent who writes; the caregiver reads.

So: the older grandparent is served by print, the younger one by the link, and neither is served
by asking them to install anything. That is a product decision, and it is already the architecture
— what is missing is the share step that makes it true in practice, which is the largest gap left
before launch.

## 2. The partnership question is a different question

Koru Kids and Bright Horizons will not evaluate this on how it looks. They will evaluate it on
liability, data protection and whether it makes their placements go better.

A childcare agency placing a nanny in a home carries real exposure when a child's allergy is not
communicated. Anything that provably improves that transfer is interesting to them; anything that
adds a system their carers have to maintain is not. The two things this product should lead with
in that conversation are exactly the two that were built first and are hardest to copy:

- **Safety-critical information is never paraphrased, never translated in place and never touched
  by a model.** It is returned in the parent's own words, verbatim, and the guide refuses to
  render at all if the check finds something was lost. That is an auditable claim, not a
  marketing one, and it is the claim an agency's insurer cares about.
- **The assistant answers only from what the parent wrote, cites it, and refuses when it cannot.**
  A carer asking a question in their own language at 2am gets the parent's answer or no answer.
  There is no third outcome where it invents one.

Neither of those is visual. Both of them are the reason to take the meeting.

There is also a harder truth about the shape of the deal. An agency partnership is a
business-to-business product: it needs an agency-side view, carer onboarding, per-placement
provisioning and a data-processing agreement, and it will be evaluated on a procurement timeline
by people who are not the user. That is a materially different product surface from the one being
built, and building it on spec — before a single agency has said yes — is how a consumer product
loses a year.

**The recommendation is to launch direct-to-consumer and treat partnerships as distribution for a
product that already works.** Not because partnerships are wrong, but because the order matters:
an agency will ask how many households already use it, and the answer has to be a number rather
than a plan. Nothing in the direct-to-consumer build forecloses the agency version — the same
household model, the same guide, the same safety contract — so this is a sequencing decision, not
a fork.

## 3. Where the visual work actually pays

Being visually appealing is not what wins a Koru Kids meeting, but it is not decorative either.
It buys two specific things:

- **The parent's confidence in sending it.** Nobody forwards something embarrassing to their
  mother-in-law. The guide is a document the parent puts their name to, and it has to look like
  something they would.
- **The recipient's first ten seconds.** A caregiver who opens the link and sees something calm
  and legible reads it. One who sees a wall of red closes it. That was the substance of the
  feedback on the safety block, and it was correct.

Both are about the reader, not the buyer. That is the right reason to do the work, and it is why
this round spent its effort on folding the safety block, filtering the guide to one subject at a
time, and moving the palette off the four apps it was colliding with — rather than on making the
product look more impressive to someone evaluating it in a pitch.
